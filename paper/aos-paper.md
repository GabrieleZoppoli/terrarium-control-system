# Cloud Forest in a Box: Growing Highland Orchids with Real-Time Weather Simulation

**Author**: Gabriele Zoppoli

*Gabriele Zoppoli is a physician-scientist — MD, PhD, and Associate Professor of Internal Medicine at the University of Genoa, Italy. Away from the hospital he maintains a living collection of more than 380 accessions of carnivorous plants, miniature orchids, and Tillandsia in Genoa, with the cloud-forest species housed in the open-source, weather-driven highland cabinet described in this article.*

---

In April 2026 a *Sophronitis coccinea* 'Big One' × 'Hinomaru' 4N GM/WOC — one of the few orchids carrying a grand-master World Orchid Conference award — opened in the upper tier of my cabinet, the brilliant orange-red flower hanging above the mounted *Dracula* and *Masdevallia* in the deep shade two shelves below. The plant has been on cork for two years and has flowered in the cabinet's continuous, weather-driven climate. The same cabinet currently maintains 75 living accessions across 31 plant genera — orchids from the Andes, the Brazilian Atlantic Forest, the New Guinea highlands, and the Philippines growing side by side with Venezuelan tepui carnivores and Sumatran, Sulawesi, and Philippine highland pitcher plants. That this collection lives, grows, and (in the case of the *Sophronitis*, *Dracula simia*, *D. lotax*, and several others) flowers under one set of conditions is the practical case for the broader idea this article is about: tropical cloud forests on three continents overlap in a climatic envelope tight enough that a single enclosure tuned to it works for plants that accept year-round moisture.

The cabinet is four years old (built May 2022). It uses real-time weather rather than fixed setpoints. Specifically, it pulls live conditions from four Colombian highland cities — Chinchiná, Medellín, Bogotá, and Sonsón, between 1,300 and 2,600 m — and uses their conditions to set its own targets. A 15-hour backward look at the archived data, combined with the 7-hour time-zone offset between Italy (UTC+2) and Colombia (UTC−5), keeps the cabinet's day/night cycle phase-aligned with Italian local time: when it is mid-afternoon in Colombia (and the cabinet is reading that data), it is mid-day in Genoa; when Colombian pre-dawn lows are arriving, it is the middle of an Italian night. The cabinet experiences a stochastic tropical-highland day driven by real weather, not a fixed schedule, but on the right clock. The cloud forests these plants come from are not flat numbers — they are *weather*, sudden temperature drops with afternoon storms, fog rolling in at dusk, brief sunny clearings at midday, seasonal shifts in cloud cover — and the cabinet plays the song, rather than holding a single note.

## The Convergent Cloud-Forest Concept

The idea behind this cabinet is not new but is underappreciated: tropical cloud forests around the world settle into surprisingly similar climates. Stand on a Colombian mountaintop at 2,000 m, an Atlantic Forest ridge in the Brazilian Serra do Mar, or a foggy slope in central New Guinea highlands, and the readings come out close: cool (10–22 °C), persistently humid (above 80 %), often in fog, with moderate light filtered through cloud. The trick is that tropical mountain meteorology — adiabatic cooling on a moist parcel as it rises, orographic lifting against the slope, cloud deck forming at a roughly fixed condensation height — produces similar microclimates at similar elevations, regardless of which mountain you are on.

For an orchid grower, that means the geographic-isolation story matters less for cultivation than the climatic envelope. Ecuadorian *Dracula simia* and Colombian *D. pholeodytes*, New Guinea *Dendrobium cyanocentrum* and *D. hellwigianum*, and Brazilian *Sophronitis coccinea* and *Laelia ghillanyi* evolved millions of years apart on different continents — but their native cloud forests overlap closely in temperature, humidity, and fog exposure. In my cabinet they grow side by side with Venezuelan *Heliamphora* and highland *Nepenthes* from Sumatra, Sulawesi, and the Philippines. Four years of co-cultivation is the practical argument for the overlap — a grower's record, not a controlled proof.

A genuinely separate thread is the *light* requirement. Tepui *Heliamphora* and Brazilian rupicolous *Sophronitis* grow in the open; *Dracula* and *Masdevallia* are deep-shade understory plants. A single overhead light cannot give both groups the same exposure — but because brightness falls off with distance from the lamps, the cabinet's three growing tiers see meaningfully different light levels, brightest at the top and a small fraction of that on the floor. The point is a coarse three-tier light gradient that lets one fixture serve plants with very different light needs, by placement rather than by separate lamps. (Direct light measurement at each tier is still on my to-do list; the figures here are descriptive, not metered.)

## A Cabinet With No Dry Season

The single most consequential decision I made about this cabinet is one of omission: it never has a dry season. Most growers of *Cattleya*-alliance orchids, many *Dendrobium*, and a long list of other genera depend on a pronounced dry, sometimes cool, rest after the pseudobulbs mature — withholding water for weeks or months to trigger flowering. That dry rest is, for those plants, not optional; it is how they bloom.

My cabinet refuses to give it. The whole population it is built around — *Dracula*, *Masdevallia*, the smaller Pleurothallidinae, highland *Nepenthes*, *Heliamphora*, *Utricularia* — are moisture-dependent plants that would suffer or die in a genuine dry-down. A cloud forest does not have a dry season in the sense a *Cattleya* grower means; the fog and the wet substrate are year-round. So I made a deliberate trade: the cabinet holds high humidity all year and protects the moisture-dependent majority, and in doing so it gives up the dry-rest flowering trigger that some of its other residents would prefer. This is, as far as I know, an unusual choice to state out loud. Most enthusiast growers either keep dry-rest plants and accept that moisture-lovers will not thrive alongside them, or vice versa, without naming the trade as a design principle.

What this looks like in practice is honest to report. The two color forms of *Cattleya walkeriana* and the *C. aclandiae* hybrid I took in as deliberate experiments are alive and putting out growth, but they have not flowered, which is exactly what the textbook predicts for *Cattleya* relatives denied a dry rest. I have not tried to push the species with the strongest dry-rest cues at all; most of the broader *Cattleya* alliance and the dry-rest *Dendrobium* (section *Callista* and the like) were left out of the cabinet pre-emptively rather than tested and lost. The cabinet is not a universal orchid house and is not meant to be. It is a cloud-forest cabinet, and the discipline that makes it work for the moisture-lovers is the same discipline that excludes the dry-rest bloomers. If your priority species need a dry rest to flower, this is the wrong system for them; if they are moisture-dependent montane miniatures, the year-round wet is the whole point.

## The Setup

The cabinet is a ~1 m³ acrylic box (1.5 × 0.6 × 1.1 m, W × D × H) sitting on a heavy aluminium scaffold, with sliding front panels for access and a layer of insulation (extruded polystyrene, faced with reflective Mylar) wrapped around the outside. Inside, a perforated acrylic shelf at mid-height splits the volume into three growing tiers:

- **Upper tier** (closest to the LEDs): the brightest position. Home to *Heliamphora*, the rupicolous *Sophronitis* and *Laelia*, and the Cattleya-s.s. miniatures that tolerate high light.
- **Middle tier** (around shelf level, intermediate): hanging baskets of highland *Nepenthes*, mounted *Utricularia quelchii*, and mid-light *Dendrobium*.
- **Lower tier** (below the shelf, low light, coolest): *Dracula*, *Masdevallia*, *Restrepia*, the small Pleurothallidinae, and the *Phragmipedium*.

Cooling comes from a small marine refrigeration compressor mounted *above* the cabinet, feeding a stainless-steel evaporator plate mounted *horizontally* on the rear wall near the floor. Cold air sinks to the bottom and rises by convection as it warms — which is why the deepest, dimmest tier is also reliably the coolest, a happy accident I have come to design around. Misting is a diaphragm pump pushing water up to a ring of nozzles on the ceiling. A small computer runs the whole thing, with no daily intervention required; the hardware details belong to the engineering paper, and what matters for the plants is the three-tier climate it produces.

## Why Real Weather, Not a Fixed Setting

What makes this cabinet different from a thermostat-and-humidistat box is that it plays the day's actual highland weather rather than holding a single set of numbers. Four Colombian highland cities (Chinchiná ~1,300 m, Medellín ~1,500 m, Sonsón ~2,475 m, Bogotá ~2,640 m) are the source, averaged into one continuously shifting target. A 15-hour backward look at the archived data, combined with the time-zone offset, keeps the rhythm right: the cabinet warms at Italian midday (when it is reading a Colombian afternoon) and cools toward saturation in the small hours (when it is reading a Colombian pre-dawn), so the plants get a real diurnal swing on a clock that matches their lights and the room around them.

The weather *events* are where the simulation earns its name. When an afternoon rainstorm sweeps through the coffee belt around Chinchiná — something that happens regularly there — the cabinet's temperature target drops and the humidity target jumps toward saturation, which on my side becomes a burst of misting and a tighter cooling cycle: a fog-immersion event the orchids did not get from any schedule I wrote. These events are not programmed; they emerge from real weather, and they vary from day to day and season to season. (If the internet drops, the system falls back to a rolling two-week average of recent weather, so the diurnal profile continues until live data resumes.)

Lighting follows a smooth daily arc rather than an on/off step: a soft sunrise ramp, peak brightness around solar noon, and a fade to dark, with the day length set from the Colombian reference latitude (~5° N) so it changes only slightly through the year, as it would near the equator. The full control logic — the weather lookup, the lighting curve, the cooling and misting loops, the safety layers that watch over an unattended cabinet — is the subject of the companion engineering paper and is summarised at the end of this article.

### *Sophronitis*, *Laelia*, and *Cattleya* — Brazilian highland and seasonally-dry miniatures

Brazilian Atlantic Forest miniatures are well represented in the upper tier — the high-light end of the cabinet, where the rupicolous group belongs. These rupicolous "*Sophronitis*" (former *Sophronitis* sensu stricto, now folded into *Cattleya* under current RHS nomenclature but still called *Sophronitis* by almost every grower of them) currently include *S. coccinea* f. *aurea*, the *S. coccinea* 'Big One' × 'Hinomaru' 4N GM/WOC of the opener, *S. brevipedunculata*, and *S. wittigiana* rosea. *S. pygmaea*, the smallest of the group, has been the difficult one: two earlier accessions — artificially-propagated plants from Großräschener Orchideen, the licensed-dealer route for Brazilian rupicolous CITES Appendix II material — were lost, and a third (a 2026 artificially-propagated import) is now establishing as a deliberate third attempt. It appears more humidity-sensitive than the other rupicolous *Sophronitis*, which have thrived. Three rupicolous *Laelia* (*L. ghillanyi*, *L. milleri*, *L. lundii* coerulea) sit alongside them, with the tiny *Isabelia pulchella* and two *Leptotes* (*bicolor* and *unicolor*) filling the same niche. The whole assembly is grown mounted on cork, dried only between mistings, never given a true rest.

The most instructive plants here are the true *Cattleya* I took in as experiments against my own design rule: the *C. aclandiae* hybrid and two color forms of *C. walkeriana* (*f. semialba* 'Tokyo No. 1' AM/AOS and *coerulea* 'Blu Monarch' × 'ABC'), all CITES Appendix II artificially-propagated material from licensed vendors, all grown under year-round moisture. These are the species the cabinet's no-dry-season rule (see "A Cabinet With No Dry Season," above) is built to exclude. They are alive and growing — slowly, no flowering yet — which is precisely the textbook prediction. I keep them as a standing demonstration of the trade rather than as an expectation of bloom: if you depend on *Cattleya*-s.s. flowering, give them their dry rest in a different setup.

### *Dendrobium* — New Guinea highlands and the Philippine representative

Five living *Dendrobium* in the cabinet, splitting into two stories. The New Guinea / section *Oxyglossum* group — the small, jewel-coloured Australasian highlands miniatures — is represented by *D. cyanocentrum* 'Blau', *D. hellwigianum*, and *D. cuthbertsonii* 'Yellow'. These are the classic high-elevation New Guinea plants, grown in the upper tier on tree-fern plaques in live *Sphagnum*; their flowers are tiny and intensely coloured, a long way from the large-flowered *Dendrobium* hybrids of the show bench. The *Oxyglossum* group is also the hardest part of the cabinet to keep: an earlier, unnamed *D. cuthbertsonii* was lost in 2022 before the 'Yellow' clone established, and this section remains the one I watch most closely. Honest cultivation reporting means saying that the New Guinea miniatures are a challenge here, not a guaranteed success.

The fifth is *Dendrobium victoriae-reginae*, the well-known "blue dendrobium." Despite the colloquial association with New Guinea, it is in fact endemic to the Philippines (POWO) and belongs to section *Calcarifera*, not *Oxyglossum* (IOSPE). It is the Philippine highland representative in the cabinet, not a New Guinea one — but its cultural needs (cool, humid, moderately bright) are close enough to the *Oxyglossum* group that it grows happily alongside them. *D. trantuanii*, also in the cabinet, is a Vietnamese highland species in yet another section, sitting in the upper tier with the others.

### *Phragmipedium kovachii*

One slipper in the cabinet, *Phragmipedium kovachii* — the famously controversial Peruvian species that arrived in cultivation in 2002 and triggered the largest CITES-related episode in modern orchid horticulture. The cabinet specimen was acquired in November 2022 from Ecuagenera (Ecuador) under the standard licensed-dealer CITES Appendix I paperwork for artificially-propagated *Phragmipedium kovachii*. It is kept in the lower tier in a sphagnum-dominated mix. *Kovachii* is moisture-loving and tolerates the cabinet's persistent high humidity well. A growing-only specimen at present; the well-documented difficulty of bringing this species to flower under cabinet conditions is part of the reason for cautious year-on-year observation rather than an expectation of bloom.

## Lessons Learned

Four years of running this cabinet have taught me several things that I think generalise to anyone trying to cultivate highland orchids outside their native range.

**The light gradient is the most valuable design feature.** Rather than trying to give every plant the same light — which would force compromises that hurt either the high-light or the shade-loving species — let the fall-off from a single overhead fixture do the work. Place *Sophronitis*, *Laelia*, and the high-light *Heliamphora* near the top; *Dracula* and *Masdevallia* on the floor; intermediate growers between. One light gives a coarse three-tier gradient that separates the cabinet into meaningfully different light levels by zone, without needing separate fixtures or a second lamp.

**Real weather feels right, even if I can't prove the plants prefer it.** Whether the orchids actively respond to the variable conditions with extra vigour or flowering is something I cannot prove without a controlled comparison, and I will not claim it. What I can say is that the cabinet feels more like a mountain than a cupboard — sudden cool spells, humidity surges, slow seasonal drift — and that the cohort has held well over four years, with many of the orchids flowering. (The single most dramatic bloom of all has been a non-orchid: a *Utricularia quelchii* that finally put up an inflorescence in April–May 2026 after three years of pure vegetative growth. I mention it because it is the kind of long-delayed surprise that a stable, naturalistic climate seems to reward.)

**Let the cooling and the airflow do separate jobs.** One practical lesson from the logs, useful to anyone building a compressor-cooled cabinet: the refrigeration does essentially all of the temperature work, and the fans are better thought of as humidity and air-movement tools than as a cooling system. Keeping those two jobs separate in your own head — and in the controller — makes the whole thing easier to reason about. The quantitative version of this, including how much the fans actually move humidity, is in the companion engineering paper.

**Pick the cohort with eyes open.** The no-dry-season trade described earlier sets which orchids belong here; the corollary in four years of running the cabinet is that survival within the moisture-tolerant cohort is *not* explained by a single cause. The losses have been a heterogeneous mix — *Sophronitis pygmaea* (humidity sensitivity), *Masdevallia coccinea* 'Anchota' and *M. glandulosa* (temperature/humidity mismatches), a *Laelia briegeri* lost not in the cabinet at all but to a CITES customs failure on import — and not a tidy "dry-rest species died" story. Climate-envelope compatibility is best read as a pre-condition for *adding* a species to the cohort, not as a guarantee of thriving once it is in. If your priority species need a dry rest, this is not the system for them; if they are moisture-dependent montane miniatures, it works well.

**Find the dead corner.** In early 2026 a faulty crimp on one of the evaporator-coil fans let one of three fans silently disconnect itself. The other two kept the cabinet on temperature, so no alarm went off — but the back-left corner stopped getting airflow, mould slowly built up on the insulation panel, and I eventually noticed because what I thought was botrytis on a *Dracula pholeodytes* bud was actually saprophytic growth living off condensation in the still corner. The full deep-clean (the first in four years) is in the companion blog post. The take-home for cabinet growers: airflow failures are often silent because the redundancy of multiple fans hides the loss of one. Look at corners.

## Resources

This article has stayed deliberately on the plant side of the cabinet. For the engineering — the weather lookup, the lighting curve, the cooling and misting control, the safety layers, the full parts list and wiring — the companion paper in *HardwareX* [1] gives the complete, reproducible design, with the control flows, firmware, dashboards, and analysis scripts released under the CERN Open Hardware Licence (a few cloud-dependent pieces, such as the weather feed and the smart-plug APIs, are noted there as external dependencies rather than original open-source work). The core hardware — a Raspberry Pi, an Arduino microcontroller, smart plugs, fans, and an LED lighting system — is commodity, and the Node-RED control logic can be inspected and modified without conventional programming.

The companion website, **https://highlandcloudforest.com**, is the living counterpart to this article: it carries the live cabinet conditions, build photographs, a per-species page for every accession named here, and blog posts on the things a static paper cannot show — the *Utricularia quelchii* first-bloom, the deep-clean episode, and the day-to-day weather events as they happen. A companion article in the *Carnivorous Plant Newsletter* [2] reports the cultivation results for the carnivores — *Heliamphora*, highland *Nepenthes*, and *Utricularia* — sharing the same cabinet.
