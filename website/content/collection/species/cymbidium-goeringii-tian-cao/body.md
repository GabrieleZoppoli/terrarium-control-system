## The cultivar

*Cymbidium goeringii* '**Tian Cao**' (天草, Japanese reading **Amakusa**) is a Japanese-line goeringii from the city of Amakusa in Kumamoto Prefecture. Compact habit, cream-margined foliage variegation that runs from edge to near the leaf tip, fragrant flowers — the variegation rule on goeringii is that any plant with leaf variegation will also produce flower variegation, so the spike colour is non-uniform.[^slippertalk]

## Growing it

Goeringii is the picky Cymbidium. Two non-negotiables: **cold dormancy with vernalisation** and a **mineral / aerated substrate**.

- **Substrate.** Hyuga pumice ~50%, Kanuma ~30%, perlite + hard pumice ~20%. Particles graded by size — coarse at the bottom, fine at the surface, with a small mound around the pseudobulb base.[^aos]
- **Pot.** Translucent or asian-style tall-narrow lets you read the moisture front at each layer. Black plastic is fine functionally; translucent is the diagnostic upgrade.
- **Watering.** Soak the pot for ~10 s with a fine spray, then let the surface mound dry out before the next thorough water. Mist between thoroughs to keep the upper third damp without flushing through. No watering during freezing weather.[^aos]
- **Vernalisation.** From the winter solstice through bud development, hold the plant at **0–14 °C** for several weeks. *Without this cold period the buds blast.* This is the single most common reason cultivated goeringii never flower in mild climates.[^orchidboard]
- **Light.** As much sun as possible up to ~40 °C. Partial shade in summer if leaf scorch shows.
- **Air movement.** Continuous gentle airflow. Goeringii roots pack densely in pots and stagnant air invites fungal/bacterial rot at the base.[^aos]
- **Repotting.** Twice a year (spring before active growth; autumn for health check) is the Asian standard. Every two to three years works in milder regimes.[^aos]
- **Fertiliser.** Light, with the growth flush; off during dormancy.

## Why bother

The genus is large; this species is the one that earned a thousand-year cultivation tradition in China and Japan. Variegated cultivars like Tian Cao were prized in Edo-period Japan for the foliage as much as the flower, and the named-cultivar lineage continues today with strict provenance.
