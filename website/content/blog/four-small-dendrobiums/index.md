This autumn, four small *Dendrobium* plants in the [highland cabinet](/highland/) are in flower at the same time. Most of them carry flowers that look too big for the plant, in yellow, violet, lilac, and a purple that turns almost blue.

Three come from New Guinea and one from the Philippines. They are related: in a DNA study of the Asian *Dendrobium*, *cuthbertsonii*, *cyanocentrum* and *victoriae-reginae* fall in the same large branch, alongside many other New Guinea species, even though *victoriae-reginae* is filed under a different section.[^tak] *Hellwigianum* was not in that study; it is placed with the New Guinea group on its flowers. In the cabinet they all get the same thing: cool nights, damp roots and air that never dries out.

## *Dendrobium cuthbertsonii* 'Yellow'

<p class="species-facts">New Guinea · 750–3,450 m · Claessen, November 2023</p>

A plant a few centimetres tall, with a flower 2–4 cm long, occasionally 5, often as long as the plant itself. The flower is lemon-yellow and waxy, with a small orange-red lip held up like a spoon. A single flower can stay open for up to eight months.[^ong]

The species lives in the New Guinea highlands, from about 750 m up to 3,450 m, high enough for night frost. Red forms probably predominate in the wild; yellow is a natural colour form, and mine is probably from a line selected for it. Ferdinand von Mueller named the species in 1888 after Cuthbertson, one of its two collectors.[^ong]

## *Dendrobium cyanocentrum* 'Blau'

<p class="species-facts">New Guinea · 100–1,300 m · Großräschener Orchideen, November 2024</p>

*Cyanocentrum* means "blue spur", after the dark blue chin at the back of the flower. The flowers are small, 1.1–1.8 cm long, with narrow, sharply pointed petals and sepals bent back; Philip Spence compared them to a diving bird.[^rw] Reeve and Woods recorded it as one of only two scented species in its section, and the scent has been compared to citronellal.[^ongnat]

Wild flowers are usually whitish with a pink, purple or blue tinge, and they tend to hide among the leaves. Mine was sold as 'Blau', a blue form, and on my mount the flowers stick out on every side, a dense violet cushion.

It is also the odd one among the New Guinea plants: it grows from about 100 m to 1,300 m, much lower and warmer than the other two. The cabinet sits at the cold edge of what it would see at home, and it doesn't seem to mind.

## *Dendrobium hellwigianum*

<p class="species-facts">New Guinea · 1,400–2,700 m · Ecuagenera, April 2024</p>

Out of flower this one is easy to overlook. The leaves are thin and round in section, and the monographers who revised the group compared them to garden chives.[^rw] The flowers are lilac, sometimes blue-grey or cream, and always end in a bright orange lip tip. They last four to six months. T.M. Reeve, co-author of the revision, saw flowers still attached to capsules that had already split open to release their seed.[^rw]

The type plant was collected by Franz Carl Hellwig in the Finisterre Mountains of New Guinea, at about 2,000 m, on 15 October 1888. Hellwig died the following year, three years before the species was published under his name in 1892.[^rw]

## *Dendrobium victoriae-reginae*

<p class="species-facts">Philippines · mostly above 1,300 m · Orchis Mundi, April 2021</p>

The one from the Philippines. Its flowers come in small clusters from old, leafless canes: white at the base, then a deep violet that goes almost blue. Cooler and brighter conditions push it towards blue.[^gbtb]

The name honours Queen Victoria. The collector August Loher published it in the *Gardeners' Chronicle* on 19 June 1897, a few days before Queen Victoria's Diamond Jubilee procession, "in commemoration of the Victorian jubilee". Not everyone was impressed: a critic in Manchester predicted that a batch of the common *D. nobile* would be "as much or more prized".[^gc] It grows in mossy mountain forest from Luzon to Mindanao, and it is listed as Vulnerable in the Philippines.

## Not one climate

I used to think of these as a set of small "cool-growing Dendrobiums" with the same needs. Their wild climates are quite different. *Cyanocentrum* grows almost down to sea level, *cuthbertsonii* up to altitudes with night frost, and *victoriae-reginae* gets a short dry spell between December and March. What they share is the cabinet: nights of 14–16 °C, days of 18–22 °C, humidity mostly between 83 % and 95 %, and no dry season at all.

None of them has that climate in the wild, and it seems to fall within what each of them tolerates. The compressor keeps the nights cool even in August, and that may be what matters most: growers report that *cuthbertsonii* suffers when nights stay above 21 °C, and Genova in August has many of those.

If the published figures hold, the *hellwigianum* flowers will last into the new year and the *cuthbertsonii* ones into spring.
